Four in five accidents are rated severity 2

Severity is the dataset's own rating from 1 (lowest) to 4 (highest). The average is 2.21. Severity 2 covers 6,156,981 records.

Recorded accidents rose every year from 2016 to 2022

2016 starts almost empty (7 records in January) and 2023 covers only January to March. The notebook does not test whether accidents increased or whether more of them were collected, so I read this as a rise in recorded accidents.

December has the most accidents and July the fewest

Totals across all years. 2020 to 2022 are the largest years and 2023 covers only January to March, so check the next slide before reading this as a seasonal pattern.

The busiest month changes from year to year

Accidents in thousands. December is the busiest month in only 2 of the 7 years that include one (2020 and 2021). July and August 2020 are far below the months around them, and the notebook does not explain why.

Accidents peak at the morning and afternoon rush hours

7 to 8 am and 4 to 5 pm hold 30% of all records. The quietest hour is 3 am, with 83,863 records. Dark bars are the five busiest hours.

California alone has 22.5% of recorded accidents

The top 3 states hold 41.5% of records. These are counts, not rates: nothing here adjusts for population or how much people drive.

The severity mix changed sharply over the years

Severity 3 falls from about a third of records in 2018 to 4% in 2022, and is absent in 2023. A shift this large in a few years more likely reflects how severity is recorded than a real change on the roads. The notebook does not test this.

Bad weather does not show higher severity

Most records by weather condition (5 of 144)

Average severity, 10 most common conditions

The highest averages are in overcast and clear conditions, and rain, snow and fog sit in the middle. All values fall between 2.13 and 2.39 on a 1 to 4 scale. Fair weather has 33% of records, but the notebook has no measure of driving per condition, so counts do not show risk.

Temperature and visibility barely change average severity

Average severity by temperature (records)

Average severity by visibility (records)

Bars use the full 1 to 4 scale, so flat bars mean little difference. Two groups are tiny (above 120°F and above 20 miles of visibility), so I do not read anything into them.

Most accidents affect a very short stretch of road

Records by distance recorded

Average severity

58% of records are 0.1 miles or less. The median is 0.03 miles, the mean 0.56 and the longest 441.75. 963,606 records (12.5%) are above the outlier limit of 1.16 miles. Longer distances do not mean higher average severity.

No weather or time factor lines up with severity

Correlation with severity, on a scale from -1 to 1. Every value is between -0.02 and 0.04. Correlation only detects straight-line relationships, and these were calculated after filling the missing weather values.

What I would do next

  1. Focus traffic monitoring on 7 to 8 am and 4 to 5 pmThese hours hold 30% of all records.
  2. Review December safety measures after checking more yearsDecember led in only 2 of 7 years.
  3. Check how severity is recorded before comparing years or statesThe mix shifts sharply across both.
  4. Compare states by rate, not by countUse population or traffic volume as the base.
  5. Improve weather and end-location data collectionLarge gaps limit what can be learned.

Proposals from this analysis. None have been put into practice.

Patterns, not causes

These are counts of recorded accidents. They show when and where records pile up, not where driving is most dangerous or why accidents happen.

Notes on the data: severity is taken as given by the dataset. Yearly and monthly counts depend on how much data was collected, and 2016 and 2023 are partial. Missing weather values were filled with state averages. Interpretations here are mine and not proven by the data.
